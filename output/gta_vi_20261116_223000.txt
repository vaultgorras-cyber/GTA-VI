The wait for Grand Theft Auto VI has always been defined by speculation, but a new variable has entered the chat: generative AI. And according to Take-Two Interactive CEO Strauss Zelnick, the publisher is already building the walls higher.

In a recent earnings call, Zelnick addressed the company’s posture on piracy heading into the launch of the most anticipated entertainment product in history. His phrasing was deliberate. Take-Two, he said, is “revving up” both its “technical defences” and its “legal activities” to combat piracy. That is the confirmed fact on the record. The publisher is investing in anti-tamper technology and preparing litigation strategies before the game even has a firm release date on PC.

The context for this urgency, however, sits in a grayer area. Reporting from Rock Paper Shotgun highlights a growing anxiety in the industry that generative AI tools could be used to reverse-engineer, upscale, or effectively “remake” console-exclusive releases into playable PC ports without authorization. The concern isn’t theoretical. We’ve already seen AI used to upscale textures, generate code, and even replicate voice acting in modding communities. The fear among publishers is that a sophisticated actor could feed a console build into an AI pipeline and produce a functional, if imperfect, PC version weeks or months before the official port arrives.

Adding weight to that theory is a comment from a former Rockstar Games technical director, Obbe Vermeij. He speculated publicly that Rockstar may move the PC release window significantly earlier than the traditional 12-to-18-month gap seen with Grand Theft Auto V and Red Dead Redemption 2. His reasoning? To undercut the financial incentive for AI-generated rip-offs. If the official PC version launches simultaneously, or close to it, the market for an unauthorized AI port collapses.

It is critical to distinguish what is confirmed from what is analysis. Zelnick’s comments on “technical defences and legal activities” are on the record. Vermeij’s insights are informed speculation from a veteran developer, not an announcement from Rockstar. And the specific threat vector — AI tools being used to automate the creation of a pirate PC port — remains a projected risk, not a documented case study. We have not seen a high-profile AAA title fully “AI-ported” to PC in the wild yet. The technology to do so cleanly, handling the immense complexity of a Rockstar engine, is unproven at scale.

What we don’t know is substantial. We don’t know what specific DRM or anti-tamper middleware Take-Two is licensing or developing internally. We don’t know if the PC version of GTA VI is targeting a day-and-date launch, a shortened window, or the traditional delay. We don’t know the legal framework Take-Two intends to use — whether they’ll target the tools, the distributors, the hosters, or the end users. And we don’t know how the modding community, a pillar of the franchise’s PC longevity, will be affected by heightened technical defences. Denuvo and similar solutions have historically drawn criticism for performance impacts and for blocking legitimate modding efforts.

There is also the broader industry backdrop. The same Rock Paper Shotgun report notes AI’s encroachment into game development, modding platforms like Nexus Mods implementing upload limits to curb AI-generated spam, and the controversial use of generative AI in a recent high-profile remake. The publisher’s stance is not happening in a vacuum; it is a defensive crouch against a shifting technological landscape.

The bottom line: Take-Two is signaling that the era of a leisurely PC port is over. They are treating piracy not as an inevitability to be managed, but as a sophisticated, AI-accelerated threat to be actively engineered against. Whether that results in a better launch experience for paying customers, or simply a more locked-down one, remains the open question. For now, the publisher has made its move. The ball is in the technology’s court.
